Ex-4-1: Communicating with Figures, with an AI Assistant

Abstract
Data Visualization

This is an in-class exercise. Before class, work through the lecture 04-1: Communicating with Figures. In class, you work through the worksheet with an AI assistant writing the code. Your job is everything the AI cannot do: deciding what each figure must say and to whom, choosing among the designs the AI offers, and judging the result in the rendered HTML.

Setup

  1. Download the worksheet, ex-4-1.qmd, and the data file county_yield.csv.
  2. Make an R Project folder called ch4-practice, with two subfolders, data and analysis. You may ask the AI how to do this.
  3. Copy county_yield.csv into ch4-practice/data, and the worksheet into ch4-practice/analysis.
  4. Open the worksheet and start at Task 1.
It worked if

Typing here::here() in the Console prints the path to ch4-practice and nothing longer, and rendering the blank worksheet produces an HTML file with one figure in it, under Task 5.

If here::here() prints a path ending in analysis, the .Rproj file was not recognised. here only accepts a .Rproj file whose first line starts with Version:, which every project created in RStudio (File, New Project) has. An empty .Rproj file made any other way does not count.

Answers

Open each one only after you have tried the task. For the design questions there is rarely one right answer. The boxes explain which choices serve the stated message and why; a different choice with a good reason is a good answer.

Task 1: Look and predict

What you should find

A. 1,957 lines: one line of column names and 1,956 data rows, 6 columns. One row is one county in one year.

B. 3 states (Colorado, Kansas, Nebraska), 154 counties (some county names, such as Lincoln, occur in more than one state, so a county is identified by its state and its name), years 2000 to 2018.

C. corn_yield runs from 0 to 234.3 bushels per acre. The 2 zeros are odd: both are in Colorado (Bent 2003 and Elbert 2002), and the next lowest yield anywhere is 80. A zero could be a crop that failed completely, or a missing value written as 0. The file cannot tell you which. Note it, and keep it in mind for any figure of yields. drought_weeks runs from 0 to 22.

D. The table of counties per state and year shows two things:

  • Colorado has no rows at all for 2009.
  • The number of counties falls over time. Kansas has 56 counties in 2000 and 2 in 2008. Colorado has 5 or fewer counties in 7 of its years.

Any average per state and year is therefore an average over a different, sometimes very small, set of counties. Task 7 comes back to this.

Task 2: Write the message

What a good answer looks like

Average yields in 2000 to 2002 and in 2016 to 2018:

State 2000 to 2002 2016 to 2018
Colorado 152 178
Kansas 159 191
Nebraska 158 205

So yields rose in all three states, most in Nebraska. A good sentence states the direction, the scope and if possible a size: “Corn yields rose in all three states between 2000 and 2018, by about 26 to 47 bushels per acre.” A sentence such as “Yields changed over time” is not a message; nothing in a figure could show it to be wrong.

The reader matters for the wording and the labels: an extension audience knows bushels per acre; a general audience needs it spelled out once.

Task 3: Ask for designs and choose

What a good answer looks like

Designs you may get back include:

  • One line per state, yearly average. Makes the rise and the comparison between states easy to see. Hides how much counties vary within a year, and each point is an average over a changing set of counties (Task 7).
  • Small panels per state, every county-year as a point, with a trend line. Shows the rise and the spread, and how few Colorado points there are. Harder to compare the states’ levels at a glance.
  • Box plots per year. Shows the spread each year, but mixes the states, so it cannot show “in all three states”.

For the sentence in Task 2, one line per state, or panels per state with a trend line, serve the message; box plots per year do not, because the message is about each state. A good written reason names what the message needs (“each state separately, over time”) and what the chosen design makes easy to see.

Task 4: Meet the figure standard

What to check in the HTML

Go through the eight items one at a time, in the HTML, not in the code:

  1. Axis titles in words with units: “Corn yield (bushels per acre)”. The years need no axis title. Nothing like corn_yield or state_name anywhere.
  2. Tick labels and axis titles at least as large as the worksheet’s text.
  3. Lines labelled at their ends, or a legend in an empty corner in the order of the lines at the right edge.
  4. Numbers: years printed as 2000, not 2,000 or 2000.0.
  5. No overlapping years along the bottom; every second or fifth year is fine.
  6. Light or no gridlines.
  7. Wider than tall, since this is a change over time.
  8. Colourblind-safe colours, one per state. If you kept colours from earlier figures, the same state has the same colour.

Check every item even though your request listed them all. A request says what you asked for, not what you got.

Task 5: Critique and improve a weak figure

What a good answer looks like

The numbers behind the message. County-years with more than 10 weeks of extreme drought, compared with county-years with none:

State Yield lower by (bushels per acre) County-years with over 10 weeks
Colorado 22 22
Kansas 17 60
Nebraska 23 69

The message is true for all three states.

The checklist, applied to the weak figure:

  1. Message in a few seconds: no. 1,956 points pile up, and most of them sit in one column at zero weeks.
  2. Colour carries information (the state), but the three states’ points are mixed together in one cloud, and where they overlap, Nebraska’s, drawn last, hide the others. The reader cannot compare the states.
  3. In black and white: no, the states become indistinguishable.
  4. Everything shown is needed, but the comparison the message needs, yields with and without long drought in each state, is not shown.
  5. Figure standard: raw column names, small text, ggplot’s default red and green, a legend to the right.

A good improvement puts the comparison the message needs in front of the reader. Two that work: one small panel per state showing yields per drought range (box plots or averages); or one point per state for “no drought” and one for “over 10 weeks”, joined by a line, so the drop is the length of the line. The first shows the spread too; the second makes the size of the drop easiest to read. Either, with a reason, is a good answer.

Task 6: Is the message true?

What you should find

2012 was a drought year everywhere: on average 12.4 weeks of extreme drought per county in Colorado, 11.5 in Kansas and 9.1 in Nebraska. But the yields did not all fall:

State 2011 2012 2013 Counties in 2012
Colorado 186 160 184 4
Kansas 161 161 175 27
Nebraska 178 186 192 64

Nebraska’s average yield was higher in 2012 than in 2011, and Kansas’s did not change, because 2011 was already a poor year in Kansas (8.4 bushels per acre below its counties’ own averages). The draft message is false for Nebraska, and no design can fix a false message. The data does not say why; it has no information on irrigation, for example.

Because the set of counties changes from year to year, a careful check compares each county with its own average over all years. That gives the same picture: in 2012, Colorado counties were 15.6 bushels per acre below their own average, Kansas counties 12.6 below, and Nebraska counties 2.6 above.

A true message, for example: “In the 2012 drought, yields were below normal in Colorado and Kansas but not in Nebraska.” It is about one year and a difference from normal, so a good design is different from Task 3’s: for example, a bar per state showing 2012’s yield minus normal, coloured on two hues either side of zero, or the Task 3 lines with 2012 marked. Colorado’s 2012 value rests on only 4 counties, which belongs in a note under the figure.

Task 7: Check the figure against its data

What you should find

For one line per state, the expected table has 3 states × 19 years = 57 rows. It has 56: Colorado 2009 is missing, and a line drawn from this table joins 2008 straight to 2010 without showing it.

The counties behind each point range from 2 to 82. Colorado’s points rest on 5 or fewer counties in 7 of its years.

What to do about it is your decision, and several answers are good, as long as the figure or its note tells the reader:

  • break the line at the missing year, so the gap is visible;
  • show the number of counties, for example as the size of each point, or in a note under the figure;
  • compare each county with its own average, so a point does not depend on which counties report that year.

Doing nothing and saying nothing is the one answer that is not good.
